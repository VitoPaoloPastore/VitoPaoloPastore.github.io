Most of the images I care about are ones a model has no business being confident about — a hundred labelled X-rays instead of a hundred thousand, microscopy of organisms nobody has catalogued, training sets where the easiest signal to learn is the wrong one. The four threads below are different angles on the same problem.

Medical imaging

Clinical images are few, acquired on different machines under different protocols, and a model that fails quietly on them is worse than no model at all.

We work on anatomical landmark detection in X-ray and MRI, lesion segmentation, and correcting motion artefacts without erasing the pathology underneath. Much of it is built on diffusion pre-training, so that a few dozen annotated scans are enough.

Biological and microscopy imaging

Biologists photograph far more organisms than anyone can label, and the interesting ones are usually the ones no classifier has seen before.

We build classifiers for plankton, cells and fungal pathogens that work without large annotated sets, and anomaly detection that flags organisms falling outside the known classes rather than forcing them into one.

Model debiasing and fairness

A network will happily learn the background instead of the object, and nothing in its accuracy on a held-out split will tell you it did.

We develop methods to discover biases nobody annotated in advance, and to remove them from models that have already learned them — including in medical imaging, where a shortcut learned from acquisition settings becomes a patient-safety problem.

Learning from limited or distributed data

Labels are expensive, data often cannot leave the hospital that produced it, and the distribution shifts between where a model is trained and where it is used.

Self-supervised and transfer pre-training, few-shot adaptation, unsupervised domain adaptation and federated learning. This thread runs through the other three rather than sitting beside them.
